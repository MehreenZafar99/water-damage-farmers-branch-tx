How Drying Actually Works

Drying is a simple cycle that repeats until materials reach normal moisture levels:

Move the air. Air movers blow across wet surfaces so moisture evaporates into the air.

Pull the water out of the air. Dehumidifiers capture that moisture and pump it out.

Keep the air warm and dry. Warm, dry air pulls more moisture out of materials.

Measure every day. Meters show whether materials are getting drier. We adjust equipment based on the numbers.

Fans alone only push damp air around. Without dehumidifiers, the moisture just moves into other parts of the property.

Classes of Water Damage

The industry sorts drying jobs by how much water got absorbed. This decides how much equipment you need.

ClassWhat it looks likeDrying difficulty
Class 1Small area, little absorption, mostly hard surfacesEasy
Class 2A whole room, wet carpet and pad, water up the walls less than 2 feetModerate
Class 3Water from above, soaked ceilings, walls, insulation, carpet, and subfloorHard
Class 4Dense materials like hardwood, plaster, brick, and concreteHardest, takes longer

Drying Methods We Use

  • Drying in place: Small holes behind baseboards let air into wall cavities without full demolition
  • Floor drying mats: Pull moisture out of hardwood floors from the top
  • Injection drying: Pushes dry air into walls, cabinets, and under flooring
  • Containment drying: Plastic walls focus dry air on the wet area
  • Slab drying: Targeted setups for concrete after slab leaks

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does structural drying take?

Most jobs take 3 to 5 days. Class 4 materials like hardwood and concrete can take a week or more.

Can I turn off the equipment at night?

Please don't. Turning it off lets humidity climb back up and slows drying.

Why does it feel warm in the room?

Dehumidifiers give off heat, and warm air helps pull moisture out of materials.

How do you know when it's dry?

We compare readings in the wet area with a dry area of the same material. Equipment stays until they match.
